Linking and Connected Speech in American English

By EnglishListing Editorial (AI-assisted, human-directed) · Updated November 13, 2026

Short answer

Native speakers don't pronounce words one by one; they join them into phrases. The main processes are consonant-to-vowel linking (an apple → a-napple), the American flap T (water → wader), dropping or softening sounds between consonants (next day → nex day), and sounds blending into each other (did you → didja). Learning them improves both listening and fluency.

“Native speakers talk too fast” is one of the most common complaints of advanced learners. Often, they aren’t actually speaking that much faster. They’re connecting words in ways the learner isn’t expecting, so word boundaries disappear. The same processes that make native speech hard to understand are the ones that make your own speech sound choppy when you don’t use them.

Linguists call this connected speech [1]. Here are the processes that matter most for American English.

1. Consonant to vowel: linking

When a word ends in a consonant and the next word starts with a vowel, the consonant moves over and starts the next word:

  • an apple → ə-NAPple
  • turn it off → tur-ni-TOFF
  • pick it up → pi-ki-TUP
  • Is it open? → i-zi-TOpen
  • check in → che-KIN

This is why learners sometimes hear words that aren’t there. An ice cream and a nice cream can sound identical.

Spanish speakers, note: Spanish links words this way too (los otros → lo-so-tros). The habit exists; you just need to let yourself do it in English, instead of separating each English word carefully.

2. Vowel to vowel: gliding

When one word ends in a vowel and the next starts with one, English slips in a tiny y or w sound to connect them, instead of stopping:

  • I am → ai-yam
  • go out → go-wout
  • the end → thee-yend
  • do it → do-wit

Without the glide, speakers often insert a small catch in the throat between the vowels, which breaks the flow.

3. The American flap T

In General American English, a T (or D) between vowels, when the following vowel is unstressed, usually becomes a quick tap of the tongue that sounds like a soft D [2]. Linguists call it a flap:

  • water → WAder
  • better → BEder
  • city → CIdy
  • meeting → MEEding
  • a lot of → ə LAdə
  • get it → GEdit
  • what about → wha-də-BOUT

The flap happens across word boundaries too (get it, what about), which is why it’s so common in fast speech. It does not happen when the T starts a stressed syllable: atTACK, iTALian, hoTEL keep a clear T.

For Spanish speakers: the American flap is very close to the Spanish single r in pero or cara. If you say water as “guára” with a Spanish tap, you’re surprisingly close. Many Spanish speakers already have this sound; they just don’t know English uses it.

4. T after N

In casual American speech, a T after N and before an unstressed vowel often disappears:

  • internet → INernet
  • twenty → TWENny
  • center → CENner
  • I want to → ai WANnə

This is informal but extremely common, even among educated speakers in professional settings.

5. Dropping sounds between consonants

When a T or D is squeezed between two other consonants, it often disappears in natural speech:

  • next day → nex DAY
  • last week → las WEEK
  • I don’t know → ai dunNO
  • exactly → igZAKly
  • facts → faks

6. Blending: D + Y, T + Y

When a word ending in D or T meets you or your, the sounds merge:

  • did you → DIDjə
  • would you → WOODjə
  • what are you → WHADdər yə or WHAchə
  • got you → GOTchə
  • meet you → MEEchə

How to practice

Listening first

You can’t produce what you can’t hear. Use Daily Dictation or the transcripts in BBC Learning English and VOA Learning English. Each time you miss a word, ask which process hid it: linking? a flap? a dropped T?

Phrase chunks

Practice common chunks as single words, not as separate words:

wha-də-yə-THINK? · LEMme-CHECK · ai-dun-NO · GEdi-DUN (get it done) · PIki-TUP

Write it the way it sounds

Take a sentence you need at work and rewrite it in “sound spelling”, as we do in this guide. Then read the sound version aloud, not the original spelling. This simple trick breaks the habit of pronouncing letters instead of sounds.

Slow it down, keep it connected

When you practice, slow down but keep the connections. Turn-it-off said slowly should still sound like tur-ni-toff, not turn · it · off. Speed will come later; connection comes first.

A note on clarity

Connected speech is not sloppy speech. Native speakers use it in board meetings and news broadcasts. But you’re in control: in a high-stakes moment (a presentation, a number, a name), you can always slow down and separate words for clarity. The goal is to have the choice, instead of being stuck with a word-by-word style.

Frequently asked questions

Why do native speakers seem to talk so fast?+

Often they aren't much faster; they connect words. Linking, reductions and the flap T remove the gaps between words, so a learner expecting separate words can't find the boundaries.

What is the flap T in American English?+

In General American English, a T between vowels before an unstressed syllable becomes a quick tap that sounds like a soft D: water, better, city, a lot of. It doesn't happen when the T starts a stressed syllable, as in attack or hotel.

Is connected speech lazy or incorrect?+

No. It's how fluent speakers talk in every context, including news broadcasts and meetings. You can always slow down and separate words for clarity when it matters, such as with numbers or names.

How do I practice linking?+

Practice common chunks as single words (turn-it-off, pick-it-up, what-do-you-think), rewrite sentences in sound spelling and read the sound version, and use dictation tools to notice the words you miss.

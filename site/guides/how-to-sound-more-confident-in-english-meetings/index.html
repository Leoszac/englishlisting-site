How to Sound More Confident in English Meetings

By EnglishListing Editorial (AI-assisted, human-directed) · Updated November 18, 2026

Short answer

Sounding confident in English meetings is mostly about delivery, not vocabulary. Finish statements with falling intonation, cut extra hedges and fillers, pause between ideas instead of rushing, stress the key word in each phrase, and prepare a few ready-made phrases for interrupting, disagreeing and asking for clarification.

Many advanced speakers know exactly what they want to say in a meeting, and still come across as less sure than they are. They have the vocabulary and the grammar. The problem is delivery: the melody, the pace and the little words around the message. The good news is that delivery is very trainable, and it doesn’t require losing your accent.

This guide covers the five delivery habits that most affect how confident you sound, plus phrases for the moments that make most people nervous.

1. End statements with a fall

The fastest way to sound unsure is to finish a statement with rising pitch. In American English, a fall at the end signals I’m done, this is my point. A rise signals a question or something unfinished. Ending statements with a rise is a known pattern called high rising terminal or “uptalk” [1]; listeners can hear it as asking for approval.

Compare (illustrative):

I think we should launch in MARCH↘. (a recommendation)

I think we should launch in MARCH↗? (sounds like you’re asking permission)

Spanish speakers sometimes carry over a rising or flat ending from their own intonation. Practice reading your key sentences with a clear drop on the last stressed word. More on pitch patterns in American intonation basics.

2. Cut the extra hedges

A hedge is a word or phrase that softens a statement, like maybe, kind of, I guess, a little bit, possibly [2]. Hedges are useful: they make you sound polite and open. But learners often stack them out of insecurity about their English:

  • ✗ Maybe I think we could possibly try to change the date, a little bit?
  • ✓ I’d suggest moving the date.
  • ✓ I think we should move the date. (one hedge, then a clear statement)

A good rule: one softener per point, then the point. Use stronger hedges only when you are genuinely uncertain.

3. Replace fillers with pauses

Fillers are sounds and words like um, uh, like, you know that fill silence while you think [3]. Everyone uses some, including native speakers. The problem is the quantity: a long string of fillers sounds nervous, while a short silent pause sounds thoughtful.

How to practice:

  1. Record yourself answering a work question for one minute.
  2. Count the fillers.
  3. Answer again, and every time you feel a filler coming, close your mouth and pause instead.

If you need time, use a real phrase instead of noise: Let me think about that for a second. / That’s a good question.

4. Slow down by chunking, not by dragging

Nervous speakers often speed up to get it over with. Instead of trying to speak slowly (which can sound unnatural), divide your sentences into short thought groups with a small pause between them:

The main ISSUE / is the BUDGET. / If we cut MARKETING / we can still hit the DEADLINE.

Short chunks and clear pauses make you easier to follow and give you time to breathe and plan the next phrase. See thought groups and pausing for the full technique.

5. Stress the word that matters

Confident speakers make the important word stand out: it’s louder, longer and higher. When every word gets equal weight, the message sounds flat and the listener has to guess what’s important.

We need the report by FRIDAY. (the deadline matters)

We need the REPORT by Friday. (not the slides, the report)

Before an important meeting, take your two or three key sentences and mark the focus word in each. Our guide to sentence stress and rhythm explains how stress works.

Phrases for the hard moments

Having ready-made phrases removes the hesitation at the moments that matter most. Practice them aloud until they come out automatically.

Getting into the conversation

  • Sorry to jump in, but…
  • Can I add something here?
  • Just to build on what Maria said…

Disagreeing politely

  • I see it a bit differently.
  • I understand the concern, but I think…
  • Can I push back on that a little?

Holding your turn when someone interrupts

  • Sorry, let me just finish this point.
  • One second, I’m almost done.

Asking for clarification (without apologizing for your English)

  • Sorry, could you say that again?
  • Just to make sure I understand: you mean…?
  • What do you mean by “scope” here?

Notice that none of these mention your English. Asking for clarification is normal in meetings; native speakers do it all the time.

Summarizing and closing

  • So, to sum up: …
  • The next step is…
  • I’ll send a summary by end of day.

A 10-minute prep routine before a meeting

  1. Write your key points in one or two short sentences each.
  2. Mark the thought-group boundaries (/) and the focus word in each group.
  3. Say them aloud three times, with a clear fall at the end of each statement.
  4. Record one take and listen: rising endings? Fillers? Rushing?
  5. Pick two phrases from the list above you might need, and say them aloud.

Practicing between meetings

  • Shadow confident speakers. Choose a short clip from a TED Talk or a business interview and copy the speaker’s pauses, stress and falling endings. See the shadowing technique.
  • Record your own calls (with permission) or rehearse updates alone and listen back.
  • Join a conversation group to practice speaking up in a group. Our guide to free English conversation practice online lists options.

The bottom line

You don’t need perfect pronunciation to sound confident. Falling endings, fewer hedges, pauses instead of fillers, clear chunking and one strong focus word per phrase will change how people hear you, often within a few weeks of practice. Frequently asked questions

Why do I sound unsure when I speak English at work?+

Common causes are rising pitch at the end of statements (which sounds like a question), too many hedges like 'maybe I think possibly', fillers, speaking fast to get it over with, and flat stress where the important word doesn't stand out.

How can I stop saying 'um' so much?+

Replace fillers with short silent pauses. A one-second pause sounds thoughtful; a string of 'um' sounds nervous. Practice by recording yourself answering questions and deliberately pausing instead of filling.

How do I interrupt politely in an English meeting?+

Use a short signal phrase with a clear voice: 'Sorry to jump in, but…', 'Can I add something here?' or 'Just to build on that…'. Say it at a natural pause, at normal volume, and keep going once you've started.

Is it bad to have an accent in meetings?+

No. Accents are normal in international workplaces. What affects how confident and clear you sound is mostly prosody: intonation, stress, pausing and pace. Those can be practiced without trying to remove your accent.

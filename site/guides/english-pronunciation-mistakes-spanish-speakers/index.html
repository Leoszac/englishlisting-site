English Pronunciation Habits Spanish Speakers Carry Over

By EnglishListing Editorial (AI-assisted, human-directed) · Updated November 13, 2026

Short answer

Spanish has 5 vowel sounds while English has many more, so Spanish speakers often merge pairs like ship/sheep and full/fool. Other common habits: adding an 'e' before s + consonant (eschool), giving every syllable equal length, keeping full vowels in weak words, a Spanish B/V, and D/T sounds where English uses TH. Rhythm usually matters more than any single sound.

If your first language is Spanish, your English accent is not random. It comes from a predictable set of differences between the two sound systems. That’s good news: predictable problems have specific fixes. This guide covers the habits that matter most, roughly in order of how much they affect how easily you’re understood.

A note before we start: having an accent is fine. Millions of people speak excellent, fully professional English with a Spanish accent. The goal here is to remove the habits that make listeners work harder, not to erase who you are.

1. Rhythm: every syllable the same length

This is the big one, and the one most people never work on. Spanish is traditionally described as syllable-timed: syllables have roughly similar length. English is stress-timed: stressed syllables are long and strong, unstressed ones are squeezed [3].

When a Spanish speaker gives every English syllable equal time, the result sounds choppy or “machine-gun” to an English ear, and the important words don’t stand out. This affects understanding more than any single vowel.

Fix: learn sentence stress and rhythm and schwa and weak forms. Practice tapping the beat on content words.

2. Full vowels in weak words

Spanish never reduces vowels: para, de, con always sound the same. In English, small words like to, for, of, and, can, the are usually reduced to a quick schwa (tə, fər, əv, ən, kən, thə). Saying them with full vowels is one of the clearest markers of a Spanish accent.

Fix: practice the weak forms in phrases: ə LOT əv PEOple, ai kən HELP, THIS is fər YOU.

3. Five vowels for a much bigger vowel system

Spanish has five vowel sounds: a, e, i, o, u [1]. General American English has many more distinct vowel sounds, depending on how you count [2]. Spanish speakers naturally map several English vowels onto the nearest Spanish one, which merges pairs that English keeps apart:

English pair What happens Tip
ship / sheep both become Spanish i sheep: long, tense, smile. ship: short, relaxed, mouth slightly more open
full / fool both become Spanish u fool: long, lips rounded. full: short, lips relaxed
cat / cut / cot all drift toward Spanish a cat: jaw down, lips spread (between a and e). cut: short relaxed uh. cot: open ah
bed / bad both become Spanish e bad: open your jaw much more
coat / caught Spanish o for both coat: glides to u (kowt)

The most socially risky mergers are the ones that create other words (sheet, beach, focus), so it’s worth practicing those pairs specifically.

Fix: minimal-pair drills (ManyThings has free listening tests), and watch the mouth position in Sounds of Speech. Remember that English vowels also change length: the vowel in stressed syllables is longer.

4. The “e” before S + consonant

Spanish words don’t start with s + consonant, so Spanish speakers often add a vowel at the start: eschool, espeak, estudent, España → Espain. Inserting an extra sound like this is called epenthesis [4].

Fix: start with a long hiss, then add the word: sssss-chool, sssss-peak. Or link from the previous word: this school → thi-SCHOOL, where the S naturally belongs to the previous word.

5. Final consonants and clusters

Spanish words mostly end in a vowel or in a few consonants (n, s, l, r, d, z). English is full of final clusters: asked, months, texts, world, helped. Spanish speakers tend to drop sounds (aks for asked) or add a vowel (helpe).

The -ed ending is a special case. It has three pronunciations, and only one adds a syllable:

  • t after voiceless sounds: helped = helpt, asked = askt
  • d after voiced sounds: called = cawld, played = playd
  • id only after T or D: wanted, needed

Saying help-ed or ask-ed as two syllables is a very common habit.

6. B and V

In Spanish, b and v are the same sound. In English, they’re different: B uses both lips; V uses the top teeth on the lower lip. Mixing them makes very sound like berry and vote like boat.

Fix: look in a mirror. For V, your top teeth should touch your lower lip and you should feel a buzz.

7. TH sounds

Spanish speakers (especially from Latin America) often replace English TH with T, D or S: tink (think), dis (this), sank you. Speakers from much of Spain have the voiceless TH in cerveza, which helps with think.

Fix: put the tip of your tongue lightly between your teeth and blow air. Think is voiceless (just air); this is voiced (add a buzz). TH is very common in function words (the, this, that, they), but because those words are usually unstressed, a slightly imperfect TH matters less than rhythm.

8. The American R and the H

The Spanish tapped or trilled R sounds very different from the American R, where the tongue doesn’t touch anything and the lips round slightly. Meanwhile, the English H is a soft breath, lighter than the Spanish j, so hello shouldn’t sound like jello.

Bonus: the Spanish single tap in pero is almost exactly the American flap T in water (wader) and better. You already have that sound. See linking and connected speech.

9. Y and J (yes vs Jess)

In many Spanish varieties, y and ll can sound like an English J (or like sh in Rioplatense Spanish). In English, yes and Jess, yet and jet, use and juice are different words.

Fix: for English Y, keep the tongue from touching the roof of your mouth; it’s a soft glide, like the i in Spanish bien, not a buzz.

10. Flat intonation

Neutral Spanish statements often use a narrower pitch range than English. Carried into English, this can sound bored or uncertain. English uses a clear pitch jump on the most important word and a clear fall at the end of statements. See American intonation basics.

Where to start

If you’re short on time, this order gives the most benefit per minute:

  1. Rhythm and weak forms (habits 1 and 2)
  2. Word stress (see word stress in English)
  3. The vowel pairs that create other words (habit 3)
  4. Final -ed and -s endings (habit 5)
  5. Everything else, as needed

Then put it together daily with shadowing. To hear the difference between a Spanish accent and an American one on the same text, compare speakers on the Speech Accent Archive.

Frequently asked questions

What is the hardest part of English pronunciation for Spanish speakers?+

Rhythm and vowels. Spanish gives syllables similar length and has five vowel sounds; English compresses unstressed syllables and has many more vowels. Fixing rhythm and weak forms usually helps more than any single consonant.

Why do Spanish speakers say 'eschool' and 'espeak'?+

Spanish words don't begin with s + consonant, so speakers add a vowel at the start. The fix is to start with a long hiss (sss-chool) or link the S to the previous word (thi-school).

How do I say the difference between ship and sheep?+

Sheep has a long, tense vowel with the lips spread; ship has a short, relaxed vowel with the mouth slightly more open. Practice minimal pairs and exaggerate the length difference at first.

Do Spanish speakers already have the American flap T sound?+

Yes. The Spanish single tap in pero or cara is very close to the American flap in water and better. Many Spanish speakers just don't know English uses it.

What Is Adult Basic Education (ABE)? Free Adult Education Classes Explained

By EnglishListing Editorial (AI-assisted, human-directed) · Updated November 18, 2026

Short answer

Adult education in the US is a network of mostly free or low-cost programs for adults 16 and older, run by school districts, colleges, libraries and nonprofits. It usually includes Adult Basic Education (ABE: reading, writing and math), English as a Second Language (ESL), high school equivalency prep (GED or HiSET) and job training. Many get federal funding under WIOA Title II.

If you’ve searched for English or GED classes in the US, you’ve probably seen acronyms like ABE, AEL, ESL, ESOL or ASE. They all belong to the same system: adult education, a network of public and nonprofit programs that help adults improve their reading, writing, math and English, often for free. This guide explains what each term means, who the programs are for and how to sign up.

What adult education is

Adult education programs serve adults who left school, never had the chance to finish, or are learning English as a new language. They’re run by:

  • school districts (often called “adult schools” or “community education”),
  • community colleges (in “non-credit” or “workforce” divisions),
  • public libraries, and
  • nonprofits such as literacy councils and immigrant-serving organizations.

Much of this system receives federal money through Title II of the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA), also called the Adult Education and Family Literacy Act. It supports services that help adults become literate in English and develop the basic skills needed for work and further education [1]. That public funding is a big reason why so many programs are free or very cheap.

The acronyms, decoded

Term Means Who it’s for
ABE Adult Basic Education Adults improving basic reading, writing and math
ASE Adult Secondary Education Adults working at a high school level, usually preparing for a high school equivalency test
ESL / ESOL English as a Second Language / English for Speakers of Other Languages Adults whose first language isn’t English
ELA / ELL English Language Acquisition / English Language Learners Other names for ESL programs and students
AEL Adult Education and Literacy The umbrella name used in some states (for example, Texas)
HSE / GED / HiSET High School Equivalency Tests and prep classes for a credential equivalent to a high school diploma
IET Integrated Education and Training Classes that combine basic skills or English with job training
IELCE Integrated English Literacy and Civics Education English classes combined with civics and workforce preparation

About the GED

The GED is a group of four subject tests (language arts, math, science and social studies) that certifies knowledge equivalent to a high school diploma; HiSET is an alternative [2]. Adult education programs typically offer free GED prep classes, though the test itself may have a fee depending on your state [2].

What it costs

Costs vary by program, but the pattern is consistent:

  • Free: many school districts, libraries and nonprofits.
  • Free for residents: some programs are free only for people who live in a certain city, county or state. For example, Tarrant County College in Texas states that all its adult education classes are free to residents of Erath, Hood, Johnson, Somervell and Tarrant counties [3].
  • Low-cost: some charge a small registration, materials or testing fee. Many will waive it if you can’t pay.

How enrollment works

Most programs follow similar steps:

  1. Contact the program or fill out its interest form. Many programs have fixed registration periods, so ask when the next one starts.
  2. Attend an orientation. You’ll learn about classes, schedules and expectations.
  3. Take a placement test. Common tests are CASAS and TABE; Tarrant County College, for instance, builds each student’s plan from CASAS scores [3]. The test isn’t pass/fail: it just finds the right level.
  4. Start class at your level, in person or online.
  5. Retest periodically to measure progress and move up.

Tip: bring a photo ID and, if the program is for local residents, proof of address. Some programs may ask other questions about eligibility; each program sets its own requirements.

ESL in adult education: what to expect

For English learners, adult education ESL classes usually:

  • go from beginner to advanced, with levels decided by the placement test,
  • focus on practical English: work, health, school, shopping, the community,
  • often meet 2–4 times per week, mornings or evenings,
  • may include citizenship preparation (see free citizenship classes),
  • sometimes offer tracks for professionals with degrees from their home country.

If you’re already advanced and your goal is to sound more natural, adult ESL may feel too basic. Combine conversation groups with our accent training guides instead.

Frequently asked questions

What does ABE stand for?+

ABE stands for Adult Basic Education: classes for adults who want to improve basic reading, writing and math skills, often as a step toward a high school equivalency diploma, a job or college.

Are adult education classes free?+

Many are free or very low-cost, especially programs run by school districts, community colleges and libraries. Some charge a small registration or testing fee, and some are free only for residents of a city, county or state. Always check the program's own page.

What is the difference between ABE and ESL?+

ABE is for adults who already speak English but want to strengthen reading, writing and math. ESL (also called ESOL) is for adults whose first language isn't English. Many programs offer both and place you with a test.

How do I enroll in adult education classes?+

Find a program near you, contact it or fill out its interest form, attend an orientation, and take a placement test (often CASAS or TABE). Then you're placed in a class at your level. Our directory lists verified free programs by city.

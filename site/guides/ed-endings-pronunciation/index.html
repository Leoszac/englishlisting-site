How to Pronounce -ED Endings: T, D or ID (With Simple Rules)

By EnglishListing Editorial (AI-assisted, human-directed) · Updated November 13, 2026

Short answer

The past-tense -ed ending is pronounced three ways depending on the sound before it: /ɪd/ (an extra syllable) after T or D (wanted, needed); /t/ after other voiceless sounds like p, k, s, sh, ch, f (worked, stopped); and /d/ after voiced sounds and vowels (played, called). The most common learner mistake is adding an extra syllable to every -ed word.

Worked, played, wanted. All three end in -ed, and all three end in a different sound. If you pronounce worked as wor-ked, with two syllables, it’s one of the most common and most noticeable learner habits, and it’s easy to fix with one rule.

The rule in one table

The pronunciation of -ed depends on the last sound of the base verb (not the last letter) [1]:

Last sound of the verb -ed sounds like Extra syllable? Examples
T or D /ɪd/ (id) Yes want → WANtid, need → NEEdid, decide → deCIdid
Voiceless: p, k, f, s, sh, ch, x, voiceless th /t/ No work → workt, stop → stopt, laugh → laft, miss → mist, watch → watcht
Voiced: b, g, v, z, j, m, n, l, r, voiced th, and all vowels /d/ No play → playd, call → calld, move → moovd, clean → cleend, love → luvd

That’s it. Only the first row adds a syllable. Worked, stopped, played, called, moved and cleaned are all one syllable.

Why the rule works

It isn’t arbitrary. It follows the way English puts sounds together [2]:

  • Voicing matches. After a voiceless sound (no vibration in the throat), the ending is voiceless /t/. After a voiced sound, it’s voiced /d/ [3]. Try saying stopd: your throat has to switch on mid-cluster, which is awkward. Stopt flows.
  • The extra vowel separates two similar sounds. After T or D, adding another T or D would be impossible to hear (want-t, need-d), so English inserts a short vowel: wan-tid, nee-did.

You don’t need to memorize the theory, but understanding it makes the rule feel natural.

Voiced or voiceless? The throat test

Put your fingers on your throat and make the sound alone (not the letter name):

  • sssss → no vibration → voiceless → missed = /t/
  • zzzzz → vibration → voiced → buzzed = /d/
  • fffff → no vibration → laughed = /t/
  • vvvvv → vibration → loved = /d/

Remember it’s about sound, not spelling: laugh ends in an F sound (voiceless) → laughed = laft. Fix ends in a KS sound → fixed = fikst.

Mistakes Spanish speakers often make

Spanish words rarely end in consonant clusters like -kt, -pt or -vd, so many Spanish speakers handle -ed in one of two ways:

  1. Adding a syllable everywhere: work-ed, play-ed, call-ed. This changes the rhythm of the whole sentence and can make simple past verbs sound like adjectives (a learned person uses the two-syllable form, I learned doesn’t).
  2. Dropping the ending: Yesterday I work late. The listener may think you’re speaking in the present.

The fix for both is the same: practice the final cluster as part of the same syllable. Start with the base verb, then add the ending without a vowel: work → work-t → workt.

Practice lists

Say each word as one syllable:

  • /t/: asked, helped, liked, looked, missed, passed, stopped, talked, walked, washed, watched, worked
  • /d/: called, cleaned, closed, lived, loved, moved, opened, played, stayed, tried, used, showed

Say each word with an extra syllable:

  • /ɪd/: added, decided, ended, expected, invited, needed, started, visited, waited, wanted

Practice sentences (illustrative)

Watch for the linking: the final /t/ or /d/ often links to a following vowel (see linking and connected speech).

  • I worked all day. → ai wor-KTALL day
  • She called a friend. → she cal-də FRIEND
  • We started at nine. → we STARti-dət NINE
  • They moved in last week. → they moov-DIN las week
  • He asked about it. → he as-tə-BOUdit

Special cases

A few adjectives ending in -ed keep the old two-syllable pronunciation, even after sounds that would normally give /t/ or /d/: naked, wicked, rugged, beloved, learned (meaning “very educated”), aged (as in an aged wine). They’re exceptions; learn them as vocabulary.

Irregular verbs (went, made, thought) don’t use -ed at all, so this rule doesn’t apply to them.

In fast speech

When a word ending in /t/ or /d/ is followed by a consonant, the ending often almost disappears: worked late can sound like work-late, called me like cal-me. Native listeners use context to fill it in. This is why -ed is hard to hear in movies. When you speak, still pronounce it clearly, especially before vowels and at the end of a sentence.

Frequently asked questions

How many ways can you pronounce -ed?+

Three: /t/ as in worked, /d/ as in played, and /ɪd/ (an extra syllable) as in wanted. Which one you use depends on the last sound of the base verb, not its spelling.

When does -ed add an extra syllable?+

Only when the verb already ends in a T or D sound: want → wanted, need → needed, decide → decided. In every other case, -ed is just a consonant added to the same syllable: worked is one syllable, not two.

How do I know if a sound is voiced or voiceless?+

Touch your throat and say the sound alone. If you feel vibration (b, g, v, z, m, n, l, r, and all vowels), it's voiced and -ed sounds like /d/. If you feel nothing (p, k, f, s, sh, ch, voiceless th), it's voiceless and -ed sounds like /t/.

Why is it hard to hear -ed in fast speech?+

Because /t/ and /d/ often get absorbed into the next word, especially before a consonant: worked late can sound like work late. Native speakers rely on context too, but in clear speech you should still pronounce the ending.

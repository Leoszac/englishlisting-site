The American T: Flap T, Stop T and Silent T Explained

By EnglishListing Editorial (AI-assisted, human-directed) · Updated November 24, 2026

Short answer

In American English the letter T has four common pronunciations. A clear T at the start of stressed syllables (top, return); a flap T, like a quick soft D, between vowels when the next syllable is unstressed (water, city, a lot of); a stop or glottal T before N or at the end of a phrase (button, what); and a dropped T after N (interview, twenty).

Few sounds change as much in American English as the letter T. In top it’s a strong, clear T. In water it sounds almost like a D. In button it nearly disappears into a little catch in the throat. And in interview many Americans don’t say it at all.

For advanced learners this matters in two ways. First, listening: if you expect a clear T in every word, phrases like a lot of or get it are hard to recognize. Second, sounding natural: a strong, clear T everywhere makes careful English sound stiff to American ears. The good news for Spanish speakers is that the trickiest version, the flap T, is a sound you already use every day.

The four American T sounds

Type Where it happens Examples Sounds like
Clear T Start of a word or a stressed syllable top, ten, return, attack A strong T with a puff of air
Flap T Between vowels, before an unstressed syllable water, city, better, a lot of A quick soft D (like Spanish r in pero)
Stop T Before N, at the end of a phrase, before consonants button, what, that guy A short catch in the throat
Dropped T After N in an unstressed syllable interview, twenty, center No T at all

These are tendencies, not laws. People vary, and careful speech uses more clear Ts. But they describe the way most Americans talk in everyday conversation.

1. The clear T

At the start of a word, or at the start of a stressed syllable, American English uses a clear T with a small puff of air (aspiration): time, today, attention, hotel. Hold a piece of paper in front of your mouth and say top: the paper should move.

Spanish T has no puff of air, and the tongue touches the teeth. For the English clear T, put the tip of the tongue a little farther back, on the ridge behind the upper teeth, and release it with air. Without the puff, English listeners may hear top as dop.

2. The flap T (water, city, better)

This is the famous American T. When T comes between two vowel sounds and the next syllable is unstressed, the tongue just taps the ridge behind the teeth once, very fast [1]. The result sounds like a soft, quick D. Flapping is one of the features that distinguishes North American English from many British accents [3].

  • water → “wa-der”
  • city → “ci-dy”
  • better → “be-der”
  • meeting → “mee-ding”
  • beautiful → “beau-di-ful”

Spanish speakers, here’s your advantage: the flap is almost exactly the single r in pero, cara or para [1]. Say pero, then say better with the same tongue movement in the middle. That’s the American flap T.

The flap T across words

The flap also happens between words when a word ends in T and the next word starts with a vowel:

  • a lot of → “a lo-dov”
  • get it → “ge-dit”
  • what about → “wha-da-bout”
  • put it on → “pu-di-don”
  • not at all → “no-da-dall”

This is why fast American English seems full of D sounds. Our guide to linking and connected speech explains how words join together.

When the flap does NOT happen

The rule depends on stress. If the T starts a stressed syllable, it stays clear, even between vowels:

  • atTACK, reTURN, hoTEL (clear T: the stress is on the syllable with T)
  • BETter, CIty, WAter (flap T: the stress is before the T)

Not sure where the stress is? See our guide to word stress in English.

3. The stop T (button, what)

Before an N sound, at the end of a phrase, or before another consonant, many Americans don’t release the T. Instead they close the throat for a moment, a sound called a glottal stop [2]. It’s the little catch in the middle of uh-oh.

  • button → “bu’n”
  • certain → “cer’n”
  • Manhattan → “Manha’n”
  • What? → “wha’”
  • that guy → “tha’ guy”
  • right now → “righ’ now”

To practice button: say but, stop your breath suddenly without releasing the tongue, then go straight to an n sound through your nose. Don’t add a vowel between them: not “bu-ten” but “bu’-n”.

At the end of a sentence, you’ll still hear the T as a sudden stop, even though the tongue doesn’t release with air. The word still sounds complete to native listeners.

4. The dropped T (interview, twenty)

After N, when the syllable is unstressed, many Americans drop the T completely:

  • interview → “innerview”
  • twenty → “twenny”
  • center → “cenner”
  • internet → “innernet”
  • wanted → “wanned”

This is common in relaxed speech and less common in formal speaking. You don’t need to produce it, but you need to recognize it: twenty and twenny are the same word.

Quick decision guide

Ask these questions when you see a T:

  1. Is it at the start of a word or a stressed syllable? → Clear T (top, attack).
  2. Is it between vowels, before an unstressed syllable or across a word boundary? → Flap T (water, a lot of).
  3. Is it before N, at the end of a phrase or before a consonant? → Stop T (button, what).
  4. Is it after N in an unstressed syllable? → Often dropped (twenty, interview).

Practice sentences

Say each sentence slowly, then at natural speed. Mark the flaps (F), stops (S) and dropped Ts (D) first.

  • I bought a lot of water at the meeting. (bought-a F, lot-of F, water F, meeting F)
  • Put it on the table, not on the button. (put-it F, button S)
  • What a beautiful city! (what-a F, beautiful F, city F)
  • I’ve got twenty interviews this winter. (got S, twenty D, interviews D, winter D)
  • Wait a minute, I’ll get it. (wait-a F, minute S, get-it F)

How to train it

  1. Listen first. Search water, button or twenty on YouGlish and listen to many American speakers say them in real sentences.
  2. Shadow short clips. Pick 20 seconds of an American podcast and copy the Ts exactly. See our shadowing technique guide.
  3. Record yourself. Read the practice sentences, record them with your phone and compare with a native recording.
  4. Start with high-frequency phrases. A lot of, get it, what about, a little bit: these come up in almost every conversation, so they’re the fastest win.

Don’t overdo it

A flap T in the wrong place (aDDack for attack) sounds strange, and a clear T is never wrong. If you’re unsure, use a clear T. The flap is a finishing touch for speakers who already have good rhythm and stress, not the first thing to fix. For the bigger picture, read why advanced speakers still sound foreign.

Frequently asked questions

Why do Americans pronounce T like D?+

Between vowels, when the following syllable is unstressed, American English turns T into a flap: a very quick tap of the tongue that sounds like a soft D. That's why water sounds like 'wadder' and city like 'ciddy'. It's a normal, standard feature of American speech.

Is the flap T the same as the Spanish R?+

Very close. The flap T is a single quick tap of the tongue behind the upper teeth, like the single r in Spanish pero or cara. Spanish speakers already have this sound; they just need to use it for T in the right places.

When is the T silent in American English?+

Often after N in an unstressed syllable, as in interview ('innerview'), twenty ('twenny') and center ('cenner'). It's also commonly dropped between consonants in fast speech, as in mostly or exactly.

Do I have to use the flap T?+

No. A clear T is always understood. But using the flap T makes your English sound more natural and relaxed to American listeners, and knowing the rules helps you understand native speakers.

B vs V in English: How Spanish Speakers Can Hear and Say the Difference

By EnglishListing Editorial (AI-assisted, human-directed) · Updated November 24, 2026

Short answer

In Spanish, the letters B and V are pronounced the same way, so Spanish speakers often say very like berry. In English they are two different sounds. B is made with both lips closing and opening (bat, best). V is made with the upper teeth lightly touching the lower lip while the voice continues (vat, vest). Practicing minimal pairs is the fastest fix.

Ask a Spanish speaker how to pronounce vaca and baca and they’ll say them the same way. That’s correct: in Spanish, the letters B and V represent the same sound [1]. The merger of the two sounds even has a name, betacism [2]. Spanish children learn to spell them as “b de burro” and “v de vaca” precisely because they sound alike.

English is different. B and V are two separate sounds, and swapping them can change the word: berry and very, ban and van, best and vest. This guide shows you how to hear the difference, how to make each sound, and how to practice until it’s automatic.

What’s happening in Spanish

Spanish has one sound for both letters, and it changes depending on its position [1]:

  • At the start of a phrase or after M/N, the lips close completely: bueno, vino, también, enviar. This is a “hard” B.
  • Between vowels and in most other positions, the lips come close together but don’t fully close, so air keeps flowing: cabeza, uva, lobo, nuevo. This is a “soft” B.

Neither of these Spanish sounds uses the teeth. That’s the key point: the English V is made with the teeth, and that’s the new movement you need to learn.

How to make the English B

English B always closes both lips completely, then releases them:

  • bat, best, boy, about, rubber, job

The challenge for Spanish speakers is the B in the middle of words. Spanish habit makes the lips stay slightly open, so rubber or above can sound soft or unclear. In English, close your lips firmly every time, even between vowels. Practice: a-bout, ba-by, ro-bot, num-ber, pressing the lips together on each B.

How to make the English V

English V is a labiodental sound: the upper teeth touch the lower lip [3].

  1. Rest your upper front teeth lightly on the inside of your lower lip. Don’t bite.
  2. Push air out through the small gap between teeth and lip.
  3. Add your voice. You should feel a buzz on your lower lip.

V is the voiced partner of F, which Spanish already has. So start from F: say fffff, then add voice without moving your mouth: fffff-vvvvv. That buzzing sound is the English V. Try fan → van, fine → vine, safe → save.

Check yourself in a mirror. For B, you should see both lips close. For V, you should see your upper teeth on your lower lip. If you can’t see your teeth when you say very, you’re still saying berry.

Minimal pairs: B vs V

Practice these pairs out loud. Exaggerate at first: hold the V buzz for a second.

berry very
ban van
best vest

Also watch: V vs F and V vs W

Once you’re making V with your teeth, two other contrasts matter:

  • V vs F (voice). Fan/van, fine/vine, leaf/leave, safe/save. The mouth position is the same; V has voice and F doesn’t. Touch your throat: you should feel vibration on V.
  • V vs W (teeth vs round lips). Some learners overcorrect and turn W into V. Wine/vine, west/vest, wet/vet. W uses rounded lips and no teeth, like the start of Spanish hueso.

Words where it matters most

Some very common English words have V, and they come up in almost every conversation. Get these right first:

  • very, have, of (pronounced with a V sound), give, live, love, over, every, never, even
  • At work: review, provide, develop, available, improve, revenue, deliver
  • Everyday: visit, video, vacation, voice, value, vote

Note that of ends in a V sound, not an F: a lot of = “a lot uv”. And have in fast speech often reduces to ’ve or uv: I should have → “I should’ve”. Our guide to weak forms and schwa explains why.

Practice sentences

Read slowly, then at normal speed. Check the V words in a mirror.

  • I have a very valuable vintage vest.
  • The best berries are available every Wednesday.
  • We voted to move the event to November.
  • Bring the video to the review, but leave the boxes.
  • Vicky believes the van is above the bank.

A 2-week plan

Days Practice (10 min/day)
1–3 F → V buzz drill; mirror check on very, have, of, every
4–7 Minimal pairs table, both columns, then mixed order
8–10 Practice sentences; record and compare with a native speaker on YouGlish
11–14 Use V words in real speech: describe your day and check very, have, every

Spelling helps

One advantage of English here: the spelling is reliable. If the word is spelled with V, it’s pronounced with a V sound; if it’s spelled with B, it’s a B. (Of is the famous exception: it’s spelled with F but pronounced with V.) So when you read, use the spelling as your guide.

Frequently asked questions

Why do Spanish speakers mix up B and V?+

Because in Spanish the letters B and V represent the same sound. Vaca and baca sound identical. English has two separate sounds, so Spanish speakers need to learn a new mouth position for V.

How do you pronounce the English V?+

Rest your upper front teeth lightly on your lower lip, then push air out while using your voice, so you feel a buzz on your lip. It's the same position as F, but with voice.

Is mixing up B and V a big problem?+

It can cause confusion between real word pairs like berry/very, ban/van and best/vest. In context listeners often understand, but a clear V makes your English easier to follow and sound more natural.

Does the English B sound like the Spanish B?+

At the start of a word, yes: both close the lips completely, as in Spanish boca. But in the middle of a word Spanish uses a softer B with the lips not fully closed. In English, B always closes the lips completely.

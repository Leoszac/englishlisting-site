The American R Sound: A Guide for Spanish Speakers

By EnglishListing Editorial (AI-assisted, human-directed) · Updated November 13, 2026

Short answer

The American R is not a tap or a trill like Spanish r and rr. The tongue never touches the roof of the mouth: it pulls back and bunches up (or curls slightly), the lips round a little, and the sound flows continuously. American English is rhotic, so R after vowels (car, work, better) is pronounced too. Spanish speakers often tap it, which sounds like a D or a flap.

For Spanish speakers, R is the letter that looks the same in both languages and sounds completely different. Spanish pero, perro and rojo use the tongue tip striking the gum ridge. American red, very and car use a sound where the tongue touches nothing at all. Getting this right changes how “American” your English sounds more than almost any other single consonant.

Spanish R vs American R

Spanish r (pero) Spanish rr (perro) American R (red)
Tongue tip Taps the ridge once Vibrates against the ridge Doesn’t touch anything
Type of sound Tap [3] Trill Approximant (continuous) [1]
Duration Very short Longer, rolling Can be held as long as you want
Lips Neutral Neutral Slightly rounded

The key difference: Spanish R is a contact sound; American R is a no-contact sound. If your tongue touches the roof of your mouth, you’re making a Spanish R.

How to make the American R

There are two tongue shapes that produce the American R, and native speakers use both [1]. Try each and keep the one that’s easier for you.

Option 1: Bunched R (often easier for Spanish speakers)

  1. Say a long eeeee.
  2. Slowly pull the whole tongue back, keeping the tip low and behind your lower teeth or floating.
  3. Let the sides of the tongue touch your upper back teeth.
  4. Round your lips a little, as if starting a w.
  5. Keep your voice on: eeee-rrrrr.

Option 2: Retroflex R (curled tip)

  1. Start with the tongue tip behind your upper front teeth, as if for an L.
  2. Slide the tip backward along the roof of your mouth, then lift it away so it floats without touching.
  3. Curl it up slightly, round the lips and voice it.

Test: you should be able to hold the R for two seconds: rrrrr-ed. If it stops immediately or “pops”, your tongue touched something.

A helpful trick: start from a growl

Many learners find it easier to start from the vowel in bird or her. Say uuuuh, then slowly pull the tongue back and up while rounding the lips a bit. The vowel turns into urrrr, like a dog’s low growl. That growl is the American R. Now put it before a vowel: rrrr-ed, rrrr-ight.

R after vowels: American English is rhotic

In General American English, R is pronounced wherever it’s written, including after vowels and at the end of words [2]: car, work, first, better, hour, world. Many British accents drop it in those positions, but American English doesn’t.

For Spanish speakers, the common problem here is the opposite of dropping it: tapping it. Car with a Spanish tap sounds like ka-ɾ, short and clipped. The American version blends the vowel and the R into one smooth sound: kaaar.

Practice the “R-colored” vowels, where vowel and R merge:

  • ER (her, work, bird, first, word, learn, Thursday): one sound, no separate vowel.
  • AR (car, start, hard, party)
  • OR (more, four, door, sport, morning)
  • EER (here, near, year, career)
  • AIR (there, where, care, chair)

Notice that work, word and world don’t have an O sound. The vowel is the same as in her: wrrk, wrrd, wrrld.

R in consonant clusters

R after another consonant (tr, dr, pr, gr, str) is where the Spanish tap sneaks in most, because tren, drama and gracias exist in Spanish with a tap. Slow down and float the tongue:

  • tree, train, try, true (the tr often sounds close to chr)
  • dream, drive, dress (the dr often sounds close to jr)
  • pretty, price, problem, proud
  • great, green, ground
  • street, strong, strategy

Your Spanish tap is still useful

Here’s the good news: the Spanish single r in pero is very close to the American flap T, the sound of the T in water, better, city and a lot of. So keep that sound; just move it from R to T between vowels. Our guide on linking and connected speech explains when the flap T happens.

Compare (illustrative):

  • ladder / latter: both flapped in American English, sounding nearly identical.
  • very (American R, no contact) vs Betty (flap T, quick contact).

Practice words and phrases

Initial R: red, right, really, read, run, room, ready, result, report, rarely

Between vowels (no tap!): very, sorry, carry, marry, period, area, story, America

Final R: car, far, near, four, your, door, where, better, teacher, computer

Phrases:

  • I really appreciate your report.
  • Are you ready for the first part?
  • Where are your four reports?
  • The rural railroad runs near the river. (a challenge)

Keep it in proportion

A Spanish R in English is noticeable, but listeners rarely misunderstand it. Fix it because it strongly affects how your accent is perceived, but keep most of your practice on word stress, rhythm and intonation, which matter more for being understood.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the American R so hard for Spanish speakers?+

Because Spanish R and RR are made by the tongue tip hitting the ridge behind the teeth (once for r, several times for rr). The American R is the opposite: the tongue doesn't touch anything. Spanish speakers have to learn to keep the tongue floating.

How do you make the American R sound?+

Pull your tongue back so the tip doesn't touch the roof of your mouth, raise the back or middle of the tongue, round your lips slightly and let your voice flow. Some speakers curl the tip up slightly instead; both shapes produce a similar sound.

Do Americans pronounce the R in car and work?+

Yes. General American English is rhotic: R is pronounced after vowels, in car, work, first, better and hour. In many British accents it isn't, which is why British speakers say 'cah' and Americans say 'car'.

Is the Spanish tap ever correct in American English?+

Yes, but for a different letter: the American flap T in water, city and better sounds very close to the Spanish single r in pero. So the sound you use for R is actually useful for T between vowels.

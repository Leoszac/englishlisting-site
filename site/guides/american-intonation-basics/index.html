American English Intonation: A Practical Guide

By EnglishListing Editorial (AI-assisted, human-directed) · Updated November 13, 2026

Short answer

In American English, each phrase usually has one focus word that gets the biggest pitch movement, normally the last content word or the word carrying new or contrasting information. Statements and wh-questions usually fall at the end; yes/no questions often rise. Speakers who keep a narrow pitch range can sound flat or uninterested even when their words are perfect.

Intonation is the melody of speech: the way your pitch rises and falls across a sentence [1]. It tells the listener what’s new, what’s important, whether you’re finished, whether you’re asking, and how you feel about it. It’s also the part of accent that learners are least aware of, because you can’t see it on the page and you can’t feel it the way you feel a tongue position.

This guide describes common patterns of General American English [3]. Real speakers vary a lot by region, personality and situation, so treat these as the default patterns that will always sound natural, not as the only correct ones.

The focus word

In each phrase, one word gets the biggest pitch movement. We’ll call it the focus word. On that word, your voice jumps up (or moves noticeably), and that syllable gets extra length.

By default, the focus goes on the last content word of the phrase:

I sent you the rePORT.

But it moves to whatever word carries new or contrasting information:

I sent YOU the report. (not someone else)

I SENT you the report. (I didn’t just mention it)

I sent you the report. (not my colleague)

Same words, four different meanings, all from one pitch jump. When a speaker puts the focus in the wrong place, the listener receives a message the speaker never meant to send. Research by Laura Hahn showed that listeners understood and remembered more when a speaker’s main stress was placed appropriately than when it was misplaced or absent [2].

Old information goes quiet

Once something has been mentioned, Americans usually de-emphasize it and move the focus elsewhere:

A: Did you like the MOVIE?

B: I LOVED the movie. (not I loved the MOVIE)

Keeping the focus on movie in the answer sounds odd to a native ear, as if B is talking about a different movie.

Falling and rising

Statements fall

At the end of a statement, the pitch rises on the focus word and then falls to the bottom of your range:

We’re meeting on TUESday. ↘

Many learners end statements without falling all the way, which can make them sound unsure or unfinished, as if more is coming.

Wh-questions usually fall too

Questions with what, where, when, why, who, how usually fall at the end, just like statements:

Where are you GOing? ↘

Rising at the end of a wh-question isn’t wrong, but it can sound like you’re asking someone to repeat something (Where are you going? ↗ = “Sorry, where did you say?”).

Yes/no questions often rise

Are you coming toNIGHT? ↗

Lists: rise, rise, fall

Each item rises except the last, which falls to show the list is complete:

We need BREAD ↗, EGGS ↗, and MILK ↘.

Choices: rise, then fall

Do you want COFfee ↗ or TEA ↘? (pick one)

Compare with a rising end, which means you’re asking if they want any drink at all.

Why you might sound flat

Many languages, including Spanish, use pitch differently and often within a narrower range in neutral speech. When those speakers carry that habit into English, Americans may hear them as bored, unfriendly or uncertain, even when they’re not. The fix is not to sound theatrical. It’s to make one clear jump per phrase, on the right word, and to fall clearly at the end of statements.

Exercises

1. Hum the melody

Take a sentence from a podcast. Listen three times, then hum it with your mouth closed, copying only the melody. Then say the words over the hum. Humming removes the distraction of sounds and spelling.

2. Move the focus

Write one sentence and say it four times, moving the focus word each time. Then explain to yourself what each version means:

SHE didn’t take the money / She DIDN’T take the money / She didn’t TAKE the money / She didn’t take the MONey

3. Question-and-answer pairs

Practice short dialogues where old information goes quiet:

  • A: Where’s the MEETing? B: The meeting’s in room FIVE.
  • A: Is it on MONday? B: No, it’s on TUESday.

4. See your pitch

If you can’t hear whether your voice moves, look at it. Praat is free software that draws your pitch line. Record a native sentence and your version, and compare the shapes. You’ll often discover that your “big” jump is tiny.

5. Exaggerate, then relax

Say a sentence with an almost cartoonish pitch jump on the focus word and a deep fall at the end. Then say it again, toning it down a little. Most learners need to feel like they’re exaggerating before they reach a normal American range.

Frequently asked questions

What is intonation in English?+

Intonation is the melody of speech: how your pitch rises and falls across a phrase. It signals what's new or important, whether you're asking or stating, whether you've finished, and your attitude.

Do questions always go up at the end in English?+

No. Yes/no questions often rise, but wh-questions (what, where, why, how) usually fall at the end, like statements. A rising wh-question can sound like you're asking someone to repeat what they said.

What is the focus word?+

It's the word in a phrase that gets the biggest pitch movement and extra length. By default it's the last content word, but it moves to whatever word carries new or contrasting information.

Why do people say I sound bored or unfriendly in English?+

If your pitch stays in a narrow range, English listeners can hear it as flat, even when you're engaged. Making one clear pitch jump per phrase on the focus word and falling clearly at the end of statements usually solves it.
